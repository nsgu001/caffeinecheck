01 The Half-Life in My Blood

Advertisement
📖

Story Narrative

Personal experiment · Data-driven · Milligram precision

The pipette slipped. Just a little. Enough to smear the calibration line across the page of my lab notebook. I was twenty‑six, sitting in a windowless room at the coffee importer’s tasting lab in Seattle, surrounded by two hundred green coffee samples. Each one tagged with a country, a lot number, a harvest date. My job was to taste, score, and log every single one. By the end of the day, my notes would include milligrams of caffeine per serving, estimated from the varietal and roast curve.

I was good at it. Too good, maybe. I could tell you within two milligrams if a natural‑processed Ethiopian had more kick than a washed Colombian. My colleagues called me “the machine.” I didn’t correct them. But I also didn’t tell them that I was mainlining five hundred milligrams of caffeine before noon most days, and that by 3 PM my hands had started to tremble over the cupping bowls.

The tremor was subtle at first. A tiny vibration in my right thumb when I reached for a sample spoon. I ignored it. Then my left eyelid began to twitch during afternoon cuppings. I told myself it was allergies. Then, one Tuesday, I poured a cup of coffee for a visiting buyer and watched the liquid slosh over the rim because my hand wouldn’t hold steady.

I set the carafe down. The ceramic made a small clink against the granite counter. I stood there for a few seconds, staring at the puddle spreading across the white stone. The buyer was still talking about pulp fermentation. I didn’t hear a word.

That night, I started a spreadsheet. Column A: date. Column B: time of day. Column C: estimated caffeine intake in milligrams. Column D: physical symptoms. The first entry: March 14, 2018, 6:15 AM, 180 mg espresso, hands steady. By the end of the week, I had fifteen rows. By the end of the month, seventy‑two. The tremor showed up on six of them, always in the afternoon, always after my third or fourth cup.

The Half‑Life Tracker is the tool I wish I had back then.

I didn’t know the science then. I knew I was tired. I knew my hands shook. I knew that at 11 PM, lying in bed in my small apartment in Capitol Hill, my heart sometimes bumped against my ribs like something trying to get out. I would lie there, staring at the ceiling, counting the seconds between beats. Sixty beats per minute. Then seventy‑five. Then ninety. Then back down.

I didn’t tell my doctor about the tremor. I didn’t tell anyone.

Here’s what I learned later, when I left the coffee industry and went back to school for food science. Caffeine doesn’t just wake you up. It enters your bloodstream within fifteen to forty‑five minutes of ingestion, peaking around an hour. Then your liver starts dismantling it. The primary tool is an enzyme called CYP1A2. It oxidizes caffeine into three different metabolites, which your kidneys then flush out.

The half‑life of caffeine in a healthy adult is about four to six hours. That means if you drink two hundred milligrams at 8 AM, you still have one hundred milligrams circulating at noon, fifty milligrams at 4 PM, and twenty‑five milligrams at 8 PM. That twenty‑five milligrams is enough to block adenosine receptors in a sensitive person. Adenosine is the chemical that makes you feel sleepy. When caffeine blocks it, you don’t just feel awake. You feel artificially awake, even when your body is exhausted.

But that’s the average half‑life. The range is enormous. Some people clear caffeine in two hours. Others take ten.

I remember the day I learned about CYP1A2 genetics. I was sitting in a food science lecture at Washington State University, three years after I’d left the coffee lab. The professor put up a slide with a graph: two curves, one labeled fast metabolizers, one labeled slow metabolizers. The fast group cleared caffeine from their bloodstream in under three hours. The slow group still had measurable caffeine in their plasma after twelve.

I stared at the slide. My hand went to my chest. The tremor was gone now—I’d cut my caffeine intake to around two hundred milligrams a day—but I remembered how it had felt. The afternoon jitters. The 11 PM heart palpitations. The nights I lay awake staring at the water stain on my ceiling.

I didn’t know my CYP1A2 status then. I still don’t. I’ve never taken a genetic test for it. But I have a strong suspicion.

A few years after that lecture, I started running small experiments on myself. I’d measure my resting heart rate before a cup of coffee and again thirty minutes later. I’d log my sleep quality using a wearable. I’d adjust my afternoon intake by fifty milligrams and see if the 3 PM tremor returned. It always did, reliably, when my total daily intake crossed about three hundred and fifty milligrams.

That’s not science. That’s n=1. But it’s my n=1.

Half‑life isn’t the only variable. There’s also the question of what happens when the caffeine wears off. Adenosine receptors don’t stay blocked forever. When the caffeine molecule finally detaches, the adenosine that’s been waiting in the wings floods in. That’s the crash. The headache. The fog. The irritability. The “I need another cup just to feel normal” cycle that millions of people live inside every day.

I spent two years inside that cycle. I didn’t know it until I stepped out.

I met Sam three years ago. She drinks tea. One cup in the morning. Sometimes she forgets to finish it. We live in a warehouse loft in Seattle with a cat named Espresso. The name was my idea. Sam rolled her eyes but didn’t veto it.

Espresso sleeps on my laptop when I’m working. She’s fourteen years old. Her kidneys are failing. She gets subcutaneous fluids twice a week. I hold her still while Sam inserts the needle. Espresso purrs through the whole thing. I don’t know if she’s happy or just too old to fight.

The first time Sam saw me pour a cup of coffee at 3 PM, she raised an eyebrow. “You know you’re going to be up late,” she said. I waved her off. That night, I tossed for two hours. She slept through it. The next morning, she didn’t say “I told you so.” She just handed me a mug of decaf and said “try this.”

I drank it. It was fine. Not great. But fine.

The sleep estimator would have told me what I learned the hard way: that a 3 PM coffee with a six‑hour half‑life still leaves a quarter of its caffeine in your system at 11 PM. For a slow metabolizer, maybe half. That’s not nothing. That’s the difference between restful sleep and restless sleep. Between dreaming and lying awake.

I still drink coffee. I’ll never stop. I love the smell of freshly ground beans. I love the hiss of the espresso machine. I love the first sip, the way it spreads warmth through my chest. But I’ve learned to stop at two cups. I’ve learned to finish my last shot before 1 PM. I’ve learned to listen to my hands.

On good days, they’re steady.

On bad days, they’re not.
