11 The Man Who Drank 6 Energy Drinks Before His Game

Advertisement
📖

Story Narrative

Personal experiment · Data-driven · Milligram precision

His name was Jack. Twenty-four years old. Amateur rugby player. Six foot two, two hundred twenty pounds. No known health problems. On a Saturday afternoon in March 2024, he drank six energy drinks in three hours. He was trying to stay alert for a tournament that had run long. The first can went down easy. The second, okay. The third, a bit much. The fourth made his stomach turn. He drank the fifth and sixth anyway.

By 5 PM, he was in the emergency room. His heart was beating at 170 beats per minute. His blood pressure was 180/110. He was vomiting. He was confused. The ER doctor asked him how much caffeine he’d had. Jack looked at his friend. His friend counted on his fingers. “Around 1,200 mg,” the friend said.

The doctor’s face didn’t change. He ordered IV fluids, activated charcoal, and a cardiac monitor. Jack spent the night in the ICU. His heart rate didn’t normalize until 2 AM.

Acute caffeine toxicity is rare, but it happens. The threshold for symptoms is around 1,200 mg for a healthy adult. That’s six energy drinks. Or four large coffees plus two energy drinks. Or two pre‑workout scoops plus a latte. The line between “alert” and “overdose” is not as wide as people think.

Jack didn’t know that. He was just tired.

I remember a case from the medical literature – a 22‑year‑old man who drank five energy drinks in two hours. He had a seizure. He survived, but he had permanent memory loss. Another case: a 19‑year‑old who mixed energy drinks with alcohol. He went into cardiac arrest. He didn’t survive.

Jack’s story ended without permanent damage. He was lucky. He stopped drinking energy drinks after that. He told me “I didn’t think it could happen to me. I’m young. I’m fit. I thought caffeine was harmless.”

The problem isn’t that caffeine is harmful. The problem is that people don’t know how much they’re consuming. A can of energy drink is a single serving, but many contain 200-300 mg. A large cold brew from a coffee shop can have 300-400 mg. A pre‑workout powder can have 400-500 mg. If you stack them – coffee in the morning, energy drink in the afternoon, pre‑workout before the gym – you can easily cross 1,000 mg without realizing it.

I’ve done the stacking myself. I’ve logged my intake on days when I was tired, stressed, or just careless. I’ve seen my total hit 800 mg, 900 mg, once over 1,000 mg. The symptoms were subtle at first – a little jittery, a little irritable. Then my heart started skipping. Then my hands shook. Then I lay in bed at 1 AM, wide awake, my mind racing.

Jack’s rugby team held a fundraiser for him after his hospitalization. They sold T‑shirts that said “Hydrate, Don’t Caffeinate.” He laughed when he showed me. “The irony,” he said. “I almost killed myself with the stuff, and now I’m a spokesperson.”

He’s not a spokesperson. He’s just a guy who learned the hard way.

The symptoms of acute caffeine toxicity include tachycardia (heart rate over 100 at rest), hypertension (blood pressure over 140/90), vomiting, diarrhea, agitation, confusion, and seizures. In severe cases, cardiac arrest. The treatment is supportive – IV fluids, activated charcoal, benzodiazepines for seizures, and sometimes hemodialysis in extreme cases.

Most people don’t need dialysis. Most people just need to stop drinking energy drinks.

I’ve thought about Jack often. Not because his case was unusual. Because it wasn’t. Young men in particular seem to treat caffeine as a performance enhancer, not a drug with a dose‑response curve. They chug energy drinks before games, before exams, before long drives. They don’t measure. They don’t track. They don’t read labels.

The FDA has considered regulating caffeine in energy drinks. In 2023, the agency issued warning letters to several companies for marketing products that were “adulterated” under the law. But no comprehensive regulation has been passed. The energy drink industry is worth billions. Lobbying is effective.

In the meantime, young people keep drinking.

I met a mother at a health fair once. She asked me what to do about her son, who drank four energy drinks a day. I asked her if he’d ever had any symptoms. She said “he’s always tired. He can’t sleep. He has terrible grades.”

I asked if she’d ever checked his caffeine intake. She hadn’t. I showed her the Half‑Life Tracker. I typed in 200 mg at 8 AM, 200 mg at noon, 200 mg at 4 PM, 200 mg at 8 PM. The curve was a mountain range. His caffeine levels never dropped below 100 mg. He was effectively caffeinated 24/7.

“That’s not normal,” she said.

“No,” I said. “It’s not.”

Her son cut back to one energy drink a day, then none. His sleep improved. His grades improved. He stopped falling asleep in class. His mother sent me an email six months later with a photo of him holding a water bottle. “This is the new him,” she wrote.

Jack still drinks coffee. One cup in the morning, sometimes none. He says he’s more sensitive now. One cup feels like two used to. His heart rate is normal. He’s back to playing rugby. He doesn’t chug anything anymore.

“I read the labels now,” he said. “Every single time.”
