Story Narrative

Personal experiment · Data-driven · Milligram precision

The scoop was larger than I expected. I’d bought the tub online after reading reviews that promised “laser focus” and “unstoppable energy.” The label said “proprietary blend.” It didn’t list the caffeine content explicitly. I had to dig through the supplement facts panel, find the fine print, do the math. 400 mg per scoop. Four hundred. That’s two energy drinks. Four cups of coffee. Half the daily recommended limit for a healthy adult, consumed in one gulp.

I didn’t take it. I sent it to a lab instead. The lab called me back a week later. “We found 420 mg,” the technician said. “Plus synephrine. Plus yohimbine. Plus a few other stimulants we can’t identify without further testing.”

I met a man named Kevin at a coffee conference in 2023. He was thirty-four, a competitive CrossFitter, sponsored by a supplement company. He told me his pre-workout routine with pride. Two scoops of pre-workout – 800 mg of caffeine. A cup of coffee – another 150 mg. A can of energy drink during his workout – 200 mg. Total: 1,150 mg. Before noon.

“You’re not working out,” I said. “You’re having a medical event.”

He laughed. I didn’t.

Kevin had been doing this for years. He said his resting heart rate was 55. His blood pressure was 120/80. He felt fine. I asked him if he ever had heart palpitations. He said “sometimes, after a heavy workout.” I asked if he’d ever mentioned that to his doctor. He said “it’s just adrenaline.”

I asked him to take a week off caffeine. Just one week. He agreed. Three days later, he texted me: “Withdrawal headache is brutal.” Six days later: “I slept nine hours last night. I haven’t slept nine hours since college.”

The combination of high-dose caffeine and intense exercise is a known cardiac stressor. A 2021 study in the Journal of the American College of Cardiology looked at young athletes who consumed high doses of caffeine before training. The researchers found that doses above 6 mg per kilogram of body weight – about 400 mg for a 150‑pound person – significantly increased heart rate variability and blood pressure during exercise. In some subjects, the combination triggered arrhythmias.

Kevin weighed 190 pounds. His 1,150 mg was 13 mg per kilogram. More than double the threshold.

Pre-workout powders are not regulated like food. They’re regulated like supplements, which means the FDA can’t take them off the market unless proven dangerous. That’s a high bar. The industry self-regulates through third‑party testing programs like NSF and Informed Sport, but not every brand participates.

I bought five popular pre‑workout powders from Amazon. I sent them to a lab. The results were all over the place. One product claimed 350 mg per serving. The lab found 410 mg. Another claimed “proprietary blend” with no caffeine amount listed. The lab found 280 mg. None of the labels disclosed other stimulants like synephrine, yohimbine, or hordenine.

Kevin had been consuming those other stimulants without knowing it.

The Half‑Life Tracker can’t account for synephrine. Neither can I. But I know that multiple stimulants have synergistic effects. 1+1 can equal 3.

I remember a client named Ashley. She was twenty‑seven, a recreational runner, training for her first marathon. She started taking a pre‑workout powder to boost her energy for early morning runs. Within three weeks, she developed chest tightness and shortness of breath during workouts. Her doctor ran tests. Everything was normal.

She came to me. I asked her to stop the pre‑workout for two weeks. She did. The symptoms disappeared. She went back on. They returned. She stopped permanently.

“I didn’t need more energy,” she told me. “I needed more sleep.”

Pre‑workout powders are marketed to young men, mostly. The tubs are large. The labels are aggressive. The names are things like “Wreckage” and “Annihilation” and “Total War.” The marketing language promises “pumps” and “focus” and “intensity.” It doesn’t mention the risk of cardiac events, sleep disruption, or withdrawal.

I’ve seen the inside of a CrossFit gym at 5 AM. The smell of sweat and chalk. The sound of barbells dropping. The row of people drinking pre‑workout from shaker bottles. Some of them are on their second scoop. Some are on their third.

I don’t judge them. I was one of them. For a brief period in my late twenties, I took pre‑workout before morning lifts. I stopped after I checked my heart rate during a rest period and found it was 120. I was sitting down.

Kevin eventually cut his caffeine intake to 400 mg per day. He still takes pre‑workout, but one scoop instead of two. No coffee on top. No energy drinks during his workout. His palpitations have stopped. His sleep has improved. He says he feels “less jittery, more focused.”

“I thought the jitters meant it was working,” he said. “Turns out, the jitters meant I was overdosing.”

If you’re taking pre‑workout, read the label carefully. Look for the caffeine amount in milligrams. If it’s not listed, consider a different brand. If it’s listed but seems high – over 300 mg per scoop – consider using half a scoop.

Your workout doesn’t need to feel like a panic attack. The gains you make from training come from recovery, not from stimulants. Caffeine can help you push harder, but it can’t help you recover faster. In fact, it might do the opposite.

Ashley ran her marathon without pre‑workout. She finished in 4 hours and 12 minutes. She said she felt strong the whole way. No chest tightness. No shortness of breath. Just the rhythm of her feet on the pavement.

“I didn’t need the powder,” she said. “I just needed to believe I could do it without it.”

The Half‑Life Tracker can’t give you belief. But it can give you data. And sometimes, data is enough.
